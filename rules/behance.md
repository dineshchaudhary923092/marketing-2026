# Craftnotion Skill — Behance Project Strategy, Writing, and Packaging

## Purpose

Use this skill whenever creating, rewriting, reviewing, sequencing, or packaging Behance projects for Craftnotion.

The goal is not to make projects look like oversized Dribbble posts. The goal is to make Craftnotion look like a serious digital product and website partner with real process, strong visual direction, and business-aware execution.

A strong Behance project should help Craftnotion do at least one of these:

- strengthen premium positioning
- build trust through depth and process
- show strategic thinking behind the visuals
- make website, SaaS, dashboard, and app work feel more credible
- improve discoverability through clearer titles, fields, and tags
- create inquiry intent from founders, operators, SMEs, and product teams

---

## Platform Reality

Behance is not primarily a caption platform.

On Behance:

- the cover gets the click
- the project structure holds attention
- the visual narrative builds authority
- the title, creative field, tags, and description improve discoverability
- the body of work does more selling than a punchy CTA ever will

Behance rewards:

- stronger presentation systems
- deeper case-study storytelling
- visual consistency
- evidence of craft and thinking
- a more complete project arc

So do not treat Behance like Dribbble with more images.

---

## Core Positioning

Craftnotion should come across as:

- a premium but practical design and development company
- strongest in websites, SaaS, dashboards, web apps, mobile apps, and MVPs
- modern, strategic, and execution-focused
- capable of both polished UI and real product thinking
- reliable for startups, SMEs, and growth-stage businesses

Craftnotion should **not** come across as:

- a random dump of UI screens
- a fashionable but shallow design profile
- a freelancer page pretending to be a studio
- a generic agency shouting adjectives
- a case-study account written only for other designers

---

## Behance Strategy Principle

Behance should show not just what was designed, but how the work thinks.

Every project should communicate some mix of:

1. category clarity
2. project context
3. visual quality
4. system thinking
5. business relevance
6. confidence in execution

The viewer should come away thinking:

- these people understand digital products
- these people can present work properly
- these people know how to move from concept to usable execution
- this is a studio, not a gallery of loose screens

---

## What Every Behance Project Must Include

Each finalized Behance project should include these items:

1. **Project Title**
2. **Cover Strategy**
3. **Creative Field**
4. **Project Tags**
5. **Project Description**
6. **Project Narrative Structure**
7. **Project Body Sequence**
8. **Closing CTA / Contact Prompt**

The exact layout can vary.
The system should not.

---

## Official Platform Constraints to Respect

When packaging a Behance project, remember:

- a project requires a **Cover Image**, **Project Title**, and at least one **Creative Field** before publishing
- Behance suggests adding **Project Tags** and **Tools Used**
- the average Behance project typically has **10–15 images** supported by descriptive text
- cover images display at **202 × 158 px**, but should be uploaded at **at least 808 × 632 px**
- project images are displayed at **1400 px wide** in project view and up to **2800 px** in lightbox view
- JPEG is recommended, PNG is accepted, and Behance recommends image files **10 MB or smaller**

Do not design or write the project like these platform realities do not exist.

## Universal Writing Rules

### Must do

- write in clear, direct English
- sound modern, confident, and commercially aware
- make the category obvious quickly
- write for both scanning and deeper reading
- support the visuals instead of duplicating them
- add enough context to show thinking, not enough to become a report
- make each section earn its place
- keep the tone premium without sounding fake-premium

### Must avoid

- long generic intros that say nothing
- repeating the same challenge-solution-result structure for every project
- dumping raw feature lists with no narrative
- empty adjectives doing all the work
- writing like a LinkedIn carousel caption
- forcing every project into the same case-study template
- burying the strongest screens too far down

## Behance Content Depth Rules

Behance needs more depth than Dribbble, but not every project needs the same amount.

### Light project
Use for:

- concept projects
- landing-page showcases
- single-direction website presentations
- section or UI system showcases

Target structure:

- strong cover
- short intro
- 6–10 visual frames
- light supporting text
- concise ending

### Standard project
Use for:

- real client websites
- SaaS showcase projects
- mobile app showcases
- dashboard systems
- product presentations

Target structure:

- strong cover
- context intro
- design direction section
- feature or flow sections
- responsive or system section
- result / takeaway / CTA

### Deep project
Use for:

- flagship product work
- multi-role platforms
- bigger systems
- authority-building case studies

Target structure:

- strong cover
- context and business goal
- problem framing
- approach / direction
- UX or IA thinking
- UI system
- key flows / modules
- responsive states or platform variations
- summary / takeaway / CTA

Rule:
**Depth should match the value of the project, not your mood on a random Tuesday.**

## Approved Project Narrative Modes

Use these modes to create variation without chaos.

### Mode 1 — Business-Led Case Study
Best for:

- service websites
- corporate websites
- premium brand sites
- landing pages with clear business goals

Approach:

- start from the business need
- show what the website needed to communicate
- explain how structure, hierarchy, and trust were improved
- keep the visual sequence purposeful

### Mode 2 — Product-Led Case Study
Best for:

- SaaS
- dashboards
- admin platforms
- fintech
- workflow systems

Approach:

- start from product complexity or workflow friction
- explain why clarity mattered
- move through the interface in logical product order
- show modules, states, panels, or flows

### Mode 3 — Experience-Led Presentation
Best for:

- mobile apps
- app flows
- login/onboarding/consumer products
- lighter product showcases

Approach:

- start from the user experience angle
- show rhythm, usability, motion, familiarity, readability
- avoid enterprise jargon unless the product actually needs it

### Mode 4 — Editorial Brand Presentation
Best for:

- niche audience platforms
- content-led projects
- faith, culture, wellness, editorial, or education work

Approach:

- open with a framing thought or thematic angle
- let the project feel more atmospheric
- keep the writing grounded and useful
- do not become poetic for no reason

### Mode 5 — System Showcase
Best for:

- design systems
- component showcases
- bento layouts
- hero sections
- visual language presentations

Approach:

- keep the text lighter
- let the visual system lead
- add enough framing to show intent

## Project Title Rules

Titles on Behance matter more than people admit.

A good title should:

- make the category obvious
- feel professional, not forced
- be searchable
- avoid bloated SEO phrasing
- sound like a real project title, not a Google Ads headline

### Best title formats

- **Brand — What It Is**
- **Brand — Industry + Experience Type**
- **Brand — Product or Website Descriptor**
- **Descriptive Title** for concept-led work

Examples:

- Tredzy — Fintech Dashboard for LC Discounting
- Speed Infra — Premium Real Estate Website
- AgentSharks — AI Website for Business Automation Solutions
- Bento Grid Web UI for a Modern Product Website

### Avoid

- vague brand-only titles when the brand is unknown
- overlong titles stuffed with every keyword possible
- titles that sound like blog headlines
- titles that say “UI/UX Design” unless that clarity is actually needed

## Cover Image Rules

The cover is one of the biggest leverage points on Behance.

### The cover must do at least 3 of these well

- show the category quickly
- look strong at a small size
- create curiosity
- feel premium and composed
- signal the project style accurately
- avoid visual clutter

### Cover image rules

- build the cover for thumbnail performance first
- do not make it dependent on small unreadable text
- do not overcrowd it with device mockups, badges, stats, gradients, and noise all at once
- use one dominant subject or composition
- make sure the first visible frame matches the project inside
- avoid bait covers that feel stronger than the rest of the project body

### Cover text rules

Allowed:

- project name
- one short descriptor
- subtle category label

Avoid:

- paragraph-length copy
- “award bait” decoration
- fake metrics
n- CTA language on the cover
- multiple competing messages

---

## Project Description Rules

Behance descriptions should support the project, not carry it.

A strong description should do 4 things:

1. explain what the project is
2. frame why the work matters
3. set up the visual narrative
4. help search/discovery naturally

### Description length

- usually 1–3 short paragraphs
- deeper only if the project genuinely deserves it
- never a wall of text before the visuals begin

### Description tone

- clear
- grounded
- commercially aware
- slightly more developed than Dribbble
- not academic, not fluffy

---

## Behance Project Body Structure

Do not use the same sequence for every project.

But in general, most strong Behance projects should use some version of this flow:

1. **Cover / Hero Frame**
2. **Intro / Context**
3. **Problem / Goal / Positioning**
4. **Visual Direction / Art Direction**
5. **Core Screens / Sections / Flows**
6. **System / Components / Responsive Views**
7. **Closing Summary / CTA**

### Optional sections

- brand framing
- user journey snapshots
- sitemap / IA
- typography / color system
- iconography
- mobile-first breakdown
- admin/user role distinction
- before/after comparison
- design rationale

Only include these if they add meaning.
Do not bolt them on to look “case-study-ish.”

## Post-Type Guidelines

## 1) Premium Website Project
Use for:

- real estate
- corporate brands
- service businesses
- premium marketing sites
- institutional websites

Emphasize:

- first impression
- hierarchy
- trust
- brand clarity
- conversion-readiness
- information flow

Do not:

- write it like a software product
- overuse “modern, clean, premium”
- make it sound like brochure copy

Recommended sections:

- intro
- business context
- hero / key sections
- page rhythm
- trust and conversion points
- responsive or supporting views

## 2) SaaS / Dashboard Project
Use for:

- fintech
- workflow tools
- admin systems
- data-heavy platforms
- portals

Emphasize:

- workflows
- visibility
- complexity management
- decision support
- user clarity
- multi-role or modular structure

Do not:

- rely on aesthetic words instead of product logic
- dump feature lists with no prioritization
- make it sound like a sales deck

Recommended sections:

- product framing
- user or role context
- dashboard overview
- key modules
- deeper screens
- system or component logic

## 3) Mobile App Project
Use for:

- app showcases
- app flows
- onboarding / login / account sections
- consumer apps

Emphasize:

- momentum
- ease of use
- screen-to-screen flow
- readability
- interaction quality

Do not:

- use overly heavy B2B language
- over-explain small app concepts

Recommended sections:

- intro
- app value / use case
- core screens
- flow highlights
- closing takeaway

## 4) Education / Content / Community Project
Use for:

- LMS
- learning apps
- editorial or content platforms
- community or membership brands
- culture / faith / niche content products

Emphasize:

- readability
- usability
- accessibility
- trust
- audience fit
- calm, structured presentation

Do not:

- sound childish
- sound generic and motivational
- oversell with “future of learning” nonsense

Recommended sections:

- thematic or audience framing
- clarity / navigation
- content or learning system
- engagement or usability points
- closing summary

## 5) Visual System / Component Project
Use for:

- design system slices
- UI systems
- hero sections
- bento grids
- component showcases

Emphasize:

- visual logic
- modularity
- consistency
- composition
- usability support

Do not:

- stretch it into a fake full case study
- invent business outcomes

Recommended sections:

- concise intro
- system frames
- component views
- usage examples
- short ending

## Behance CTA Rules

Behance does not need hard-selling CTAs.

The project itself should do most of the persuasion.

### CTA purpose

A CTA on Behance should do one of these:

- encourage profile exploration
- encourage website visits
- create inquiry intent
- frame Craftnotion as a serious execution partner

### Good CTA styles

- If you’re building something similar, let’s talk.
- Explore more of our website and product work at Craftnotion.
- Looking for a design and development partner? Let’s connect.
- More project work available through our profile and website.

### Avoid

- Hire us now
- Limited slots
- Book now
- Contact immediately
- Unlock explosive growth
- Best agency in India

Behance is not the place for panic marketing.

---

## Tags, Fields, and Discovery Rules

Behance gives you structured discovery tools. Use them properly.

### Required mindset

- choose the right **Creative Field** first
- use **Project Tags** to clarify the category, not spray keywords blindly
- make the title and description naturally searchable

### Tag mix

For most projects, use a mix of:

- category tags
- industry tags
- format tags
- audience or use-case tags

Example for a fintech dashboard:

- fintech
- dashboard design
- financial platform
- SaaS
- product design
- web app
- admin panel

### Avoid

- irrelevant style tags just because they are trendy
- duplicate tags that mean the same thing
- vanity tags with no discovery value
- stuffing every possible synonym

---

## Image and Layout Rules

### Behance image rules

- prioritize vertical reading flow
- build a consistent width rhythm across the project
- avoid jarring switches between completely different frame styles
- keep margins/padding visually intentional
- use text frames sparingly and purposefully
- place strongest images early
- do not bury the best section at frame 11

### Recommended sequence rhythm

- one strong opener
- one or two context frames
- several image-led sections
- one system or detail section
- one closing frame

### For exported frames

- do not overuse tiny text
- keep hierarchy readable on desktop and mobile
- avoid making every frame look like a poster
- make sure mockups help, not dilute

---

## Project Sequencing Rules Across the Profile

Behance profile order should tell a business story.

Lead with:

- strongest SaaS/product work
- best dashboards
- premium websites aligned to current positioning
- clean multi-screen projects that show depth

Push lower:

- weaker concept work
- experimental shots with thin business relevance
- older work that no longer represents current quality
- fragmented UI slices unless they support a system story

The profile should say:

**we do serious product and website work**
not
**we have posted many things over time**

---

## Craftnotion-Specific Positioning Rules

Across Behance projects, reinforce these ideas often:

- strategy before decoration
- clarity before noise
- usable product thinking
- scalable design systems
- conversion-aware website design
- business-aligned digital execution
- practical, premium work

Do not overuse these words:

- innovative
- cutting-edge
- disruptive
- world-class
- bespoke
- seamless
- revolutionary

A few are fine.
Too many and the copy smells like agency soup.

---

## Final Review Checklist

Before finalizing a Behance project, check:

- Does the title clearly communicate the category?
- Does the cover work as a thumbnail?
- Does the project body feel intentional, not dumped?
- Does the first third of the project carry enough strength?
- Does the writing explain the work without smothering it?
- Does the project feel premium without trying too hard?
- Does it sound like Craftnotion?
- Would a founder or product team take this seriously?
- Does it feel deeper than Dribbble, without becoming bloated?

If not, fix it.
