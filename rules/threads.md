# Skill: Craftnotion Threads Content Guidelines

## Purpose

Use this skill to create, review, and refine Threads content for Craftnotion.

The goal of Threads is not to behave like Instagram without images.
The goal is to make Craftnotion sound like a real team of builders with taste, experience, and practical thinking.

Threads should help people feel:

* this team understands digital products
* this team thinks clearly
* this team is active and human
* this team is trustworthy
* this team is worth following and eventually contacting

---

## Platform Role

Threads is the conversational layer of the brand.

It should feel:

* human
* direct
* thoughtful
* modern
* light on jargon
* less polished, more alive

Threads is where Craftnotion should sound like:

* founders
* product thinkers
* experienced builders
* honest operators

Not like:

* a brochure
* a press release
* an auto-posting agency bot

---

## Primary Objective

Every Threads post should do at least one of these:

1. reveal how Craftnotion thinks
2. show product and delivery maturity
3. build familiarity and trust
4. create curiosity around Craftnotion’s work
5. make the account worth following even before someone needs an agency

If a post does none of the above, do not publish it.

---

## What Threads Is Best For

Threads works best for:

* short opinions
* product lessons
* client misconceptions
* execution truths
* lightweight proof
* build-in-public observations
* compact advice
* follow-up thoughts on projects
* human reactions to common problems in design/development

Threads is not ideal for:

* long corporate announcements
* overdesigned portfolio packaging
* generic “we are excited to share” posts
* too much self-praise
* lifeless copy

---

## Voice Rules

### Tone should be

* sharp
* calm
* confident
* conversational
* honest
* practical

### Tone should not be

* preachy
* motivational guru style
* startup-bro loud
* overly polished
* robotic
* desperate for engagement

### Golden rule

Write like someone who has actually shipped projects and seen where things go wrong.

---

## Core Content Pillars

### 1. Builder Observations

Short posts about what repeatedly happens in real projects.

Examples:

* Most clients do not need more features first. They need better structure.
* A confusing website can make a good business look smaller than it is.
* Scope issues usually start long before development starts.

### 2. Product Lessons

Clear, useful takeaways from product, website, app, or SaaS work.

Examples:

* The fastest way to delay a product is to skip requirement clarity.
* Most MVPs fail because they try to look complete instead of proving one thing well.
* A premium design is useless if the user journey is still messy.

### 3. Proof Snippets

Very short trust posts.

Examples:

* Delivered this project in 3 weeks when other vendors quoted 6 months.
* One of the biggest things clients value is fast, honest communication.
* A lot of our work is not just design or just code. It is cleaning up product confusion.

### 4. Process Thinking

Explain how Craftnotion approaches work.

Examples:

* We treat clarity as part of delivery, not a precondition for it.
* A good admin panel is not about more controls. It is about fewer mistakes.
* We usually think about trust, structure, and speed before visual polish.

### 5. Market/Founder Reality

Useful opinions for startups, SMEs, and product owners.

Examples:

* A cheap software quote is often just expensive confusion postponed.
* Most business websites are designed for vanity, not clarity.
* Founders often ask for an app when the real problem is workflow design.

## Approved Post Types

# 1. SINGLE-THOUGHT POSTS

## Purpose

Share one clear, useful idea.

## Structure

* one idea
* one supporting line if needed
* stop

## Examples

* The difference between a decent website and a high-trust website is usually clarity, not animation.
* A lot of software delays begin in the requirements, not in the code.
* Clients rarely need more features first. They need better prioritisation.

## Rules

* no bloated explanation
* no trying to fit 5 ideas into 1 post
* should feel quotable and re-shareable

---

# 2. TWO-TO-FOUR LINE MINI POSTS

## Purpose

Add a bit more nuance while staying easy to read.

## Structure

Line 1: hook or observation
Line 2: explanation
Line 3: consequence or takeaway
Optional line 4: light CTA or question

## Example

A premium-looking website can still fail.
If the structure is messy, the trust drops immediately.
Design is not just how it looks. It is how clearly it helps someone move forward.

## Rules

* keep paragraphs short
* one point only
* avoid long walls of text

---

# 3. MINI CASE STUDY POSTS

## Purpose

Turn project work into short, native content instead of portfolio dumping.

## Structure

* what the client/business needed
* what Craftnotion focused on
* what changed

## Example

We recently worked on a website where the real issue was not visuals.
It was trust.
The business looked smaller online than it actually was.
Once the structure, hierarchy, and messaging were cleaned up, the whole brand felt more credible.

## Rules

* do not over-name-drop
* focus on lesson, not bragging
* do not write it like a caption from Instagram

---

# 4. MYTH / REALITY POSTS

## Purpose

Challenge common misunderstandings.

## Structure

* Myth:
* Reality:
* one-line explanation

## Example

Myth: a fast project is always rushed.
Reality: a clear project can move very fast.
Confusion is usually what slows delivery down.

## Rules

* keep myth believable
* keep reality practical
* avoid fake controversy

---

# 5. CLIENT MISCONCEPTION POSTS

## Purpose

Build authority by naming common misunderstandings.

## Examples

* Many founders ask for “just a simple app” before the workflow is even clear.
* A lot of people think design delays projects. Unclear decisions usually do more damage.
* Businesses often ask for more pages when the real issue is weaker messaging.

## Rules

* do not sound mocking
* stay useful
* write from experience, not ego

---

# 6. OPINION POSTS

## Purpose

Give Craftnotion a voice.

## Good opinion topics

* why cheap software gets expensive later
* why most agency communication is too vague
* why clarity matters more than complexity
* why some websites feel premium and others feel disposable

## Rules

* opinions should be defensible
* do not rage-post
* do not force hot takes for attention
* be sharp, not obnoxious

---

# 7. QUESTION POSTS

## Purpose

Invite replies without sounding like engagement bait.

## Good formats

* What do you think damages trust faster on a website: weak structure or weak copy?
* What is one thing founders usually underestimate before building an MVP?
* What makes a business website feel instantly outdated to you?

## Rules

* questions must be relevant to your domain
* avoid generic prompts like “what are you working on today?”

---

# 8. LIST POSTS

## Purpose

Deliver fast value.

## Examples

* 3 things that usually increase software cost
* 4 signs a website needs a structural rethink
* 5 mistakes we often see before a product build starts

## Rules

* keep lists short
* each point should feel worth reading
* no filler points just to hit a number

---

# 9. LIGHTWEIGHT PROOF POSTS

## Purpose

Build trust without looking like an ad.

## Examples

* One thing clients consistently appreciate: direct communication.
* A lot of our work is cleaning up unclear ideas before they become expensive builds.
* Some of our strongest projects moved fast because the decision-making was clear.

## Rules

* no chest-thumping
* proof should feel observed, not bragged
* do not repeat website claims every week

---

## Repurposing Rules

### From Instagram to Threads

Do not copy-paste carousel captions and dump them as Threads posts.

Instead, extract one thing from the post:

* the lesson
* the mistake
* the process insight
* the business implication

### From Facebook to Threads

Trim explanation heavily.
Threads needs more punch and less paragraph weight.

### From projects to Threads

Do not say:
We are excited to share our latest project.

Say:
One interesting thing about this project was...
The real challenge was...
What changed most was...

That is how native platform thinking works.

## Formatting Rules

* keep posts visually breathable
* use short lines
* line breaks are allowed
* do not dump giant blocks of text
* avoid excessive emojis
* avoid hashtag stuffing

### Hashtags

Use sparingly or not at all.
Only use when clearly relevant.
Threads content should stand without hashtag crutches.

---

## CTA Rules

Threads CTAs should be light.

Good examples:

* Curious how others see this.
* We see this a lot in product work.
* This is one of the most common mistakes we notice.
* Planning something similar?
* Would love to know if others have seen the same.

Avoid:

* DM us now
* Book a call today
* Limited slots available
* Follow for more

The account should feel worth following on merit.

---

## What To Avoid Completely

* corporate launch announcements with no insight
* auto-posted portfolio spam
* recycled Instagram copy with hashtags everywhere
* overly polished ad language
* generic startup motivation
* trend-chasing with no relevance
* engagement bait questions with no depth

---

## Post Review Checklist

Before publishing, check:

1. Does this sound human?
2. Does this sound like experience, not theory?
3. Is there one clear idea?
4. Would a founder, SME owner, or product person find this worth reading?
5. Does this strengthen trust or authority?
6. Is it short enough to feel native to Threads?
7. Is it free from brochure language?
8. Would this still be interesting even without an image?

If it fails 2 or more, revise it.

---

## Quality Standard

A good Craftnotion Threads post should feel like this:

* compact
* smart
* calm
* useful
* opinionated when needed
* grounded in real work

The account should feel like a digital product team thinking in public.
Not a social media intern filling a spreadsheet.
